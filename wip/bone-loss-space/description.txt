Guess first: one month in orbit equals how many years of aging? 👇

On Earth, the hips and spine of older people lose about 0.5 to 1% of their bone density per year.
In orbit, astronauts' weight-bearing bones (hips, spine) lose on average about 1 to 1.5% per month (these averages come mostly from missions before today's ARED weight machine; with hard exercise many crews now lose less). So one month in space is like a year or more of age-related bone loss.
Why: floating, the bones no longer carry the body's weight. The cells that build new bone slow down, while the cells that break down old bone keep their normal pace, so the bone gets weaker.
The fix: exercise. Space station crews work out for about two hours a day on a treadmill (held down by a harness), an exercise bike and a weight-lifting machine called ARED.

About the video: an original animation. The rates are averages and vary a lot between astronauts and bone sites. The bars are zoomed in (90-100%), and the bone, cells and people are illustrations, not measurements.

Sources:
Orwoll et al., "Skeletal health in long-duration astronauts", NASA Bone Summit, Journal of Bone and Mineral Research 2013 (1.0-1.5% hip and spine loss per month in flight vs 0.5-1.0% per year in older people on Earth): https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.1948
NASA, The Human Body in Space (weight-bearing bones lose on average 1% to 1.5% of mineral density per month): https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/the-human-body-in-space/
NASA, Risk of Spaceflight-Induced Bone Changes (1% to 1.5% per month on 4-6 month missions; hips and spine): https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-spaceflight-induced-bone-changes/
NASA, Counteracting Bone and Muscle Loss in Microgravity (bone-building cells slow down while bone-removing cells keep their pace; crews exercise an average of two hours a day): https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/counteracting-bone-and-muscle-loss-in-microgravity/
