How many days for a full turn? 👇

Earth isn't a perfect sphere. It spins, so the equator bulges out: the equatorial diameter is about 43 km larger than the pole-to-pole diameter (equatorial radius 6,378.137 km, polar radius 6,356.752 km). That extra mass around the middle tugs on anything orbiting at an angle.

For the ISS (about 400 km up, tilted 51.6° to the equator), the bulge slowly swings the whole orbit plane around the poles. The orbit's line of nodes drifts west about 5° per day, so the ring makes a full turn in roughly 72 days. That is one reason the station passes over different parts of the world as the days go by.

The twist: satellites use the exact same effect on purpose. Tilt the orbit near-polar (around 98°) and the bulge turns the plane about 0.9856° per day — exactly the rate Earth moves around the Sun. The orbit then stays fixed relative to the Sun and crosses the equator at the same local time every day. That's a Sun-synchronous orbit, used by most Earth-observing satellites.

About the video: an original computer animation. Earth's flattening is shown greatly exaggerated (the real flattening is only about 1/300); the orbit size is not to scale. The nodal-precession rate (about 5°/day for the ISS, a full turn in about 72 days) follows the standard formula dΩ/dt = −1.5 · n · J2 · (Rₑ/a)² · cos i (NASA GDC Orbit Primer).
